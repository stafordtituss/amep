AMEP: Verifiable Evidence That Agent Actions
Stayed Within Delegated Authority

Staford Titus Selvaraj
Independent Researcher · stafordtituss@gmail.com · ORCID 0009-0004-8227-7595
Abstract AI agents increasingly act for people through chains of sub-agents, tools and adapters. Identity tells a relying party who acted, and authorization tells it what a token allowed, but neither shows whether what was actually executed stayed within what a person authorized. We present the Agentic Mandate Evidence Protocol (AMEP), an open specification for portable, signed evidence of each link in that chain (mandate, delegation, intent, interpretation, invocation, approval and an independently observed executed effect) and a deterministic, three-valued evaluation over it. The evaluation returns preserved, violated or unresolved, and every non-preserved result names one of eleven invariants. Missing or unverifiable evidence yields unresolved, never permission. Authority is modelled as claim sets with a decidable order and meet, so attenuation across delegation hops can be checked offline by any third party with public keys alone. We describe the design, the claim algebra and invariants, and a conformance suite of 95 vectors on which two independently written verifiers agree. We also report two integrations: an MCP extension prototype in which tool servers return signed receipts of the effects they actually executed, and a 20-vector delegation suite mapped to an agent-identity registry protocol under discussion in a W3C Community Group. AMEP is published as an IETF Internet-Draft with open-source reference implementations.
Keywords: AI agents; delegation; authorization; attenuation; audit evidence; verifiable records; Model Context Protocol; capability tokens.

1. Introduction

An agent acting for a principal rarely acts directly. A user asks a host agent; the host spawns sub-agents; sub-agents call tools through adapters; adapters translate calls into effects on files, SaaS records, payment systems or other people's inboxes. At every hop, what is done can drift from what was asked. An approval for one command can be replayed for another. A sub-agent can receive broader scope than its parent held. Untrusted content, such as a README, an email or a web page, can steer a mutation. An adapter can map a declared call to a different effect. A revoked grant upstream can leave a live child downstream. And an enforcement point can say "deny" on a host that runs the action anyway.

Existing standards answer adjacent questions. OAuth 2.0 Token Exchange [1] issues delegated tokens and OpenID AuthZEN [2] transports access decisions. Transparency services such as SCITT [3] and audit-record architectures [4] make records tamper-evident. Capability tokens such as Macaroons [5], Biscuit [6] and UCAN [7] make attenuation structural. None of them states, in a form a third party can check offline, whether an executed effect stayed within the authority the principal delegated, or how to treat the common case where the evidence needed to decide is missing.

AMEP addresses that gap. Its contributions are:

  • an evidence model that separates identity, authority, declared action, executed effect and approval into separately signed objects, each signed by a key holding a role permitted for that object (Section 3);
  • a claim algebra with a decidable order (⊑), meet (⊓) and satisfaction (⊨) relation, which makes attenuation and effect containment mechanically checkable (Section 4);
  • eleven invariants and a deterministic, three-valued evaluation in which missing evidence is never permission (Section 5);
  • a public conformance suite with must-reject vectors and two independent verifiers that agree on it, plus conformance levels that separate deployment maturity from verifier interoperability (Section 6);
  • two integrations: signed effect receipts for Model Context Protocol tool calls, and delegation-continuity vectors for an agent-identity registry protocol (Section 7).

2. Problem and threat model

We consider a principal H who authorizes an agent A, which may delegate to agents B, C, …, which invoke tools T. A relying party (a receiving tool, an auditor or a regulator) wants to know, for each executed effect: whose authority it was exercised under; whether the authority narrowed or widened along the chain; whether the executed effect matched what was declared; whether any approval covered exactly that effect; and whether any link had been revoked at the time.

AMEP's guarantees are designed to hold under an untrusted-model assumption: every language model in the chain may be adversarial, and nothing a model says about itself counts as evidence. Hosts, adapters and observers are trusted only for the statements their keys are entitled to sign. We assume Ed25519 [8] is unforgeable and that verifiers obtain authentic key sets with role assignments. AMEP does not detect prompt injection, issue identities, or define a policy language. It records the findings of such systems and evaluates the evidence they leave.

Three design principles shape the rest of the paper. Declared ≠ executed: what an agent declares and what a system executed are recorded separately and compared. Fail-safe evidence: missing, unsigned or unverifiable evidence yields unresolved, never preserved. Independent observation: effects that write shared state, send data externally, destroy data or move money need an observer other than the actor.

3. Evidence model

A packet is a set of signed objects submitted together for evaluation. Each object is a JSON document signed with Ed25519 over its RFC 8785 (JCS) canonical form [9], excluding the proof member. Every key in a key set carries roles, and a verifier treats an object as unsigned unless its key holds a role permitted for the object's kind. Without roles, a compromised host key could mint its own mandate or approve its own action, and the evidence would still verify.

Two choices are central. First, the executed effect is observed, not claimed: the receipt is signed by the system where the action took effect, or by a proxy or host hook, and carries an assurance level from L0 (self-reported) to L3 (the receiving system). Second, decisions carry a phase (pre_execution, post_execution or simulation), so detection after the fact is never presented as enforcement.

4. Claim algebra

Authority is a claim set C with action patterns, resource patterns, permitted data flows (class→recipient), an effect-class ceiling from the ordered set read < write_local < write_shared < external_send < destructive < financial, a validity window, an optional purpose, deny lists, an optional risk budget and a delegation depth.

Order. C1 ⊑ C2 (the child is equal to or narrower than the parent) holds when every action and resource pattern of the child is subsumed by one of the parent's; the child's data flows are a subset of the parent's; its ceiling, expiry and budget are no greater; its not-before time is no earlier; its purpose is unchanged; its deny lists include every deny of the parent; and the depth rule holds. A parent with depth 0 may not be delegated, and a child of a parent with positive depth must carry a strictly smaller depth.

Meet. C1 ⊓ C2 is computed component-wise: patterns subsumed by the other side, intersected data flows, the lower ceiling, the tighter window, and the union of deny lists, so that no hop and no intent can lift a deny. The effective claims of an invocation are the meet of every claim set on its chain, met again with its intent when one is cited.

Satisfaction. An executed effect x satisfies C (x ⊨ C) when its action and resource match an allowed pattern and no deny pattern, its effect class is within the ceiling, every released data flow is permitted, and its time is within the window.

These relations use only pattern subsumption, set inclusion and comparison, so they are decidable and cheap. Adding a hop can never widen effective claims (monotonicity). Capability tokens enforce the same attenuation rule structurally: a widened Macaroon or Biscuit simply fails to verify. AMEP instead records each delegation as a separately signed claim set and checks ⊑ afterwards, so a widening is detected and reported as a violation with the responsible hop. The two compose: tokens can carry mandates and delegations, and AMEP profiles map their facts onto claims.

Evaluation is a pure function of the packet, the key set, pinned adapters and, for streams of packets, verifier state (approvals already used and budget already spent). For each invocation the verifier resolves the chain to a root mandate, verifies every signature and role, checks interpretation, attenuation, liveness, influence, protected resources and receipts in a fixed order, and returns the first failing invariant. Orphan effects and decisions are reconciled across the packet. The packet verdict is violated if any result is violated, otherwise unresolved if any is unresolved, otherwise preserved. Because the order and the outcome of each check are fixed, the same inputs always give the same verdict and deciding invariant, which is what allows independent verifiers to be compared vector by vector.

The three-valued outcome matters in practice. Audit pipelines that collapse "could not determine" into "allowed" turn missing logs into permission. AMEP keeps the two apart: a revocable mandate with no fresh status, a receipt signed by a key without the observer role, or a high-impact effect reported only by its actor all evaluate to unresolved, naming I7 or I10, while a provable widening or a revoked root evaluates to violated.

Worked example. A user's mandate allows fs.* on repo:app/* until t=100 with depth 2. The host delegates only fs.write on repo:app/src/* until t=90 with depth 1. The user revokes the mandate at t=5; at t=10 the sub-agent writes repo:app/src/auth.ts. The delegation is a valid attenuation (I1 holds), and the effect satisfies the effective claims (I2 holds), but the signed revocation precedes the invocation, so the verdict is violated, I6: the sub-agent's authority died with its root.

6. Conformance and implementations

The conformance suite (v0.2.0) has 95 vectors: 25 positive packets that must evaluate to preserved and 70 must-reject packets that must evaluate to a stated outcome and invariant. They cover attenuation (expand-then-shrink, depth, validity, purpose, deny removal), declared-versus-executed drift, revocation and status freshness, signer-role misuse, approval replay and time-of-check/time-of-use substitution, data release after a read, guardrail writes, self-reported high-impact effects, orphan effects and cross-packet budget accounting. Every normative change to the specification must add at least one positive and one must-reject vector that distinguish the new behaviour from the old.

Two verifiers were written independently against the specification text: one in Python and one in JavaScript, which runs under Node.js and in the browser using WebCrypto. They agree on all 95 vectors, and the vector packets validate against both JSON Schemas and CDDL. Both are published as packages (amep-protocol on PyPI, @stafordtituss/amep on npm) with producer SDKs for signing evidence.

Conformance is reported on two axes. Levels 0–3 describe where AMEP sits in a deployment: observation, mandate attached, pre-execution decision, delegated attenuation. Levels 4–5 describe what others can check: a verifier that passes the suite, and cross-vendor interoperability between two independent implementations. An "Attested" qualifier marks deployments whose high-impact effects are observed at L2 or above and registered with a transparency service.

7. Integrations

7.1 Model Context Protocol

In the Model Context Protocol (MCP) [15], tool annotations such as readOnlyHint are a server's description of itself, and tool results are unsigned, so an organization reviewing an incident cannot distinguish "the server deleted three records" from "the agent said it did". We specified an optional MCP extension in which a client or gateway attaches an invocation identifier and a mandate reference to each tools/call in _meta. A server that supports the extension returns, in the result's _meta, a signed ExecutedEffectReceipt describing what it actually executed. The extension adds no methods and changes no result content, and peers that do not advertise it behave exactly as before.

A prototype built on the official MCP Python SDK's extension interface includes a reference server, which signs receipts for its tools' real effects, and a reference gateway, which verifies the receipts and evaluates the evidence. Eight integration tests run real MCP sessions, in process and over stdio. In them an honest server yields preserved; a server that changes a different record than requested is exposed by its own receipt and yields violated (I2); a client that does not opt in receives no receipt and yields unresolved (I7); and a receipt tampered with in transit is rejected.

7.2 Delegation continuity for agent identity registries

Agent identity systems answer who an agent is; they do not by themselves answer whose authority a delegated agent exercises. We built a 20-vector suite on a hiring scenario discussed in the W3C Agent Identity Registry Protocol Community Group, extended with one delegation hop: an HR lead authorizes a hiring agent, which delegates interview scheduling for one requisition to a scheduling agent, which writes to a calendar service that signs what it did. Agents are identified by DIDs, and the registry's namespace:resource:action scopes are mapped to AMEP claims. The suite has 5 positive and 15 must-reject vectors. They cover scope widening, a raised effect class, a dropped deny, a delegation that outlives its parent, re-delegation without depth, effects outside the delegated requisition, executed-versus-declared drift, upstream revocation, stale status, identity without delegated authority, a self-issued delegation, and approval mismatch and replay. Both verifiers agree on all 20.

Building the suite surfaced two questions for identity-registry designs. First, AMEP delegations name their parent but not their holder, so binding a delegation to the delegate's identifier must come from the credential that carries it. Second, status-list freshness measured at verification time differs from liveness at the time of the action, which is what an auditor needs: a link revoked after the action leaves the action preserved, and a status older than the action leaves it unresolved.

8. Related work

Delegation and authorization. OAuth 2.0 Token Exchange [1] and transaction-token work issue delegated credentials; AuthZEN [2] standardizes decision requests. AMEP consumes these as sources of mandates and decisions and adds the check that executed effects stayed within them. Capability tokens. Macaroons [5], Biscuit [6] and UCAN [7] enforce attenuation structurally; AMEP shares their attenuation rule, detects rather than prevents widening, and covers what tokens do not record: interpretation, intent, independently observed effects and approvals bound to effects. Audit and transparency. SCITT [3] and audit-record architectures [4] make records tamper-evident and discoverable; AMEP objects can be registered as signed statements, and AMEP supplies the evaluation over such records. Agent security. AgentDojo [10] benchmarks prompt-injection robustness, and systems such as CaMeL [11], FIDES [12] and Progent [13] enforce control-flow, information-flow or privilege policies inside agents. These are complementary: they can emit AMEP evidence, and AMEP provides a portable, cross-system way to check the outcome. Verifiable credentials. AMEP evidence can be exchanged as W3C Verifiable Credentials [14] for audit, while the AMEP proofs inside determine the outcome.

9. Limitations and future work

AMEP verifies what it is given: a verifier cannot detect lineage or effects a host failed to record, which is why observer independence and transparency registration matter. Revocation in distributed deployments needs a quiescence window that v0.2 does not define. Token-profile vectors (Biscuit, Macaroon, UCAN) still require a profile-aware verifier. Verifier state across packets is local bookkeeping without a signed exchange format. A proposed EvaluationRecord, an append-only and supersedable signed record of verifier results, addresses how an unresolved result is later resolved without rewriting history. Finally, AMEP does not yet have deployment measurements; reporting overheads and coverage from production agent hosts is the next step.
